Managing migraines day to day

General information, not medical advice — everyone's migraines are different, and a doctor who knows your history is always the best judge of what's right for you.

Sleep

Irregular sleep is one of the most commonly reported migraine triggers — both too little and too much.

  • Keep roughly the same bed and wake time, even on weekends
  • Aim for a consistent 7–9 hours rather than "catching up" later
  • Notice if oversleeping on rest days tends to bring one on

Hydration & food

Dehydration and skipped meals are frequent, easy-to-miss triggers.

  • Sip water through the day rather than all at once
  • Try not to go long stretches without eating
  • Track specific foods in your log — common ones people report are aged cheese, alcohol, caffeine, and processed meats, but it's highly individual

Stress

Migraines can follow high-stress periods — or even show up right after stress lets up.

  • Build small breaks into busy days rather than pushing straight through
  • Short breathing exercises or a brief walk can help in the moment
  • If weekends or vacations tend to trigger one, that's worth logging too

Screens & light

Bright or flickering light and long screen sessions are common sensory triggers.

  • Try the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds
  • Lower screen brightness or use a warmer color mode in the evening
  • Sunglasses or dimmer indoor lighting can help if you're light-sensitive

Weather & hormones

Some people notice patterns tied to barometric pressure shifts or hormonal cycles.

  • Log weather changes as a trigger if you suspect a connection
  • If you track your cycle, logging it alongside episodes can reveal a pattern over a few months
  • Patterns like these are often only visible after logging consistently — that's what this app is for

When to see a doctor

Your log is genuinely useful to bring to an appointment — it turns "I get headaches a lot" into an actual pattern.

  • Migraines that are frequent (roughly 4+ days a month) or getting worse over time
  • Needing pain medication very often — overusing it can itself cause more headaches
  • A sudden, severe headache unlike your usual ones, or one with new neurological symptoms, needs urgent care, not a wait-and-see approach
